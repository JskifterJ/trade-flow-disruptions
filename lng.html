1 · The supply chain: from gas field to burner tip

LNG is natural gas (mostly methane) cooled to −162 °C, where it becomes a liquid taking up about 1/600th of its gaseous volume. That lets gas cross oceans where pipelines can't reach, but every molecule needs a chain of very expensive, fixed assets. 2 · Who sells, who buys, and what is exposed

Three exporters (the US, Qatar and Australia) supplied 63% of the world's LNG in 2025. Asia takes most of it, but Europe's share jumped to 30% after it lost Russian pipeline gas. How the shock gets absorbed

Who lost supply, who replaced it, in one line each
  • South Asia (Pakistan 99%, India 59%, Bangladesh) lost its main supplier and is cutting gas use or paying spot.
  • China, Taiwan, Korea bid for Australian, Malaysian and US cargoes. China leans on Russian pipeline gas.
  • Europe (only ~7% direct exposure) competes with Asia for US cargoes, so TTF rises with JKM.
  • US LNG is the swing supplier: ~123 Mt in 2026 (+14%), with new trains at Plaquemines, Corpus Christi and Golden Pass.
  • Shipping whipsawed: Atlantic→Asia voyages and Cape detours tied up ships in March, then a record wave of new carriers and short US→Europe runs pushed day rates below pre-war levels (section 5).
Sources: GIIGNL Annual Report 2026; Reuters/Kpler (Oct 2026).

3 · The supply wave, 2026–2031

More than 200 Mtpa of liquefaction is under construction, most of it in the US and Qatar. Most of it arrives in 2027–2028. That matters for traders: once Hormuz reopens and North Field East starts, the market could swing from shortage to surplus quickly. 4 · Prices and storage: what the shock did to gas markets

Through 2024–25 TTF ran at $8–15 per MMBtu and Henry Hub at $1.50–4.30. That spread pays for liquefaction and shipping (see the arbitrage below). Hormuz closing on 28 Feb 2026 and the 18 Mar strike on Ras Laffan sent TTF to $25 by September. Europe began its refill season with storage around 30% full and was still below the 2021–2025 minimum in October, so it has to outbid Asia for every spare cargo. Hover a chart for values.

5 · Fleet and freight: what it costs to move a cargo

A spot charter is a ship hired by the day. When voyages get longer or ships get stuck, fewer are free and day rates jump; when the fleet grows faster than voyages lengthen, rates collapse. The war did both. Rates spiked to nearly $290,000 a day in early March, when Qatar's ships were trapped and buyers scrambled for Atlantic cargoes. By October they were below pre-war levels: a record 107 new ships are due in 2026, US cargoes are taking the short route to Europe, and the map's legs need fewer tonne-miles than in 2025. 6 · The import side: regas capacity and how much of it is used

Import terminals are the cheap end of the chain. The world can regasify roughly three times the LNG it trades, so most terminals run part-time and the spare capacity is what lets a buyer outbid others for a cargo when a supplier fails. Utilisation tells you where that slack is: Japan and Korea built for winter peaks and run at about a third, while north-west Europe (Belgium, the Netherlands, Poland) ran at 90% or more in 2025, replacing Russian pipeline gas. Europe started about a dozen floating terminals (FSRUs) after 2022 and is still adding them. Hover the bars for each figure's date and source.

8 · The arbitrage: where does a US cargo go?

Most US LNG is sold FOB (free on board) at about 115% of Henry Hub plus a fixed liquefaction fee, so whoever owns the cargo sends it wherever the delivered margin is highest. That decision moves ships between the Atlantic and the Pacific every month. Move the sliders to see how the spread between JKM (Asia) and TTF (Europe), freight and the Panama Canal decide it.     10 · Primer: the ten things to know

    1. Liquefaction is the bottleneck. The world had 524 Mtpa of liquefaction capacity at end-2025 but 1,247 Mtpa of import (regas) capacity, about 2.4× more. Supply, not import terminals, limits trade, so when Qatar's 77 Mtpa vanished nobody could replace it quickly. More than 200 Mtpa is under construction, mainly in the US and Qatar (section 3).

    2. Trains, tanks and jetties. A plant is built in "trains" (parallel liquefaction units of ~1–8 Mtpa each). Qatar's mega-trains are 7.8 Mtpa. Newer US plants use many small modular trains. Each plant also needs pre-treatment (removing CO₂, water and sulphur), cryogenic storage tanks and a loading jetty.

    3. Ships are a scarce asset. The IGU counted 804 active LNG carriers at end-2025 (with 301 on order), far fewer than oil tankers. A standard ship carries ~174,000 m³, about 3.6 million MMBtu or 0.1 bcm of gas. Qatar's Q-Max ships carry 266,000 m³. When voyages lengthen (Cape detours, Asia instead of Europe), ships get tied up and charter rates spike; when new ships arrive faster than voyages lengthen, rates collapse (section 5).
    4. Boil-off. About 0.1–0.15% of the cargo warms and evaporates each day. Most carriers burn it as fuel, so long voyages literally cost cargo.

    5. Regas: onshore vs. floating. Onshore terminals take 3–5 years to build. A FSRU (floating storage and regasification unit) is a converted ship that can be moored in months: Germany went from zero to four operating units after 2022, with a fifth due at Stade (section 6), and Egypt, Pakistan and Bangladesh rely on them.

    6. Three pricing worlds. Henry Hub (US, ~$3), TTF (Europe) and JKM (Asia spot), all in $/MMBtu. Long-term Asian contracts are often oil-indexed (≈12–14% × Brent). The spread between hubs is what pays for shipping and what traders monetise.
    7. Contracts vs. spot. 57% of 2025 trade was on long or medium-term contracts and 43% on spot or short-term deals (35% spot alone), a share that is rising fast because US volumes are destination-free.

    8. FOB vs. DES. With FOB the buyer lifts the cargo at the plant and controls the ship (and the destination). With DES (delivered ex-ship) the seller delivers to the buyer's terminal. FOB US cargoes are what make LNG a "global" market.

    9. Chokepoints matter more for gas than oil. Oil has strategic reserves and bypass pipelines. Qatari LNG has neither, and storage at both ends is small (days to weeks in Asia), so a closed strait hits power grids within weeks.

    10. Watch these: Hormuz transit counts, Qatar's monthly loadings, the JKM–TTF spread, Panama slot auctions, EU storage fill, Golden Pass and Plaquemines ramp-ups, and the 1 Jan 2027 EU ban on Russian LNG.
